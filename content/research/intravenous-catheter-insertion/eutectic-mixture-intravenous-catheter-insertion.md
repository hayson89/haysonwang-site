Setting: A general inpatient pediatric ward.

# Eutectic Mixture of Local Anesthetics Reduces Pain During Intravenous Catheter Insertion in the Pediatric Patient

Allyson Cordoni, M.S.N., A.R.N.P., and Lance E. Cordoni, M.D.

Department of Pediatrics, Children’s Hospital of Illinois, Peoria, Illinois, U.S.A.

## Abstract:

Objective: The objective of this study was to explore the relation between the application of a mixture of lidocaine/prilocaine cream (eutectic mixture of local anesthetics [EMLA]) before intravenous cannula insertion and perceived pain in the pediatric patient.

Design: Double-blind placebo-controlled trial.

Patients: We examined 26 male and 31 female patients between the ages of 4 and 12 years who required intravenous cannula insertion. Intravenous insertion was performed on 57 patients, with 29 patients in the placebo group (mean age, 8.1 years) and 28 in the EMLA group (mean age, 8.0 years).

Interventions: Application of either EMLA cream or placebo 45 minutes before intravenous cannulation.

Outcome Measures: Pain was scored by the patients using a 0- to 10-cm visual analogue scale combined with a Faces pain scale as well as visual observation by a nurse. Adverse side effects were recorded in a separate table.

Results: Data collected and the differences between the placebo and treated groups were tested using a Mann-Whitney U test. Those children in the EMLA group (mean pain score, 1.25) experienced less pain than those in the placebo group (mean, 8.39). There was no statistical significance between age, sex, and race.

Conclusions: The authors conclude that a topical preparation of lidocaine/prilocaine significantly reduces children’s pain during intravenous cannula insertion when applied to an intact dermal layer of the skin and that this effect occurs within 45 minutes.

Key Words: EMLA—Lidocaine—Methemglobinemia.

Pain and fear of needles can make the insertion of an intravenous (IV) catheter a traumatic experience for a child. If hospital staff can provide painless IV insertion, a child is likely to be more cooperative, and venous access may be obtained more quickly. The use of an eutectic mixture of local anesthetics (EMLA) has been used successfully to reduce pain during a variety of painful procedures such as lumbar puncture,<sup>1</sup> removal of papules by curettage,<sup>2</sup> intramuscular injections,<sup>3</sup> and venipuncture.<sup>4</sup> This EMLA is formulated as a white cream consisting of 2.5% lidocaine and 2.5% prilocaine in an oil base.<sup>5</sup> The recommended application time for EMLA cream is 60 minutes before a procedure.<sup>5</sup> Previous studies involving adults and children have found application times ranging from 5 to 60 minutes to be effective in reducing pain with phlebotomy.<sup>6,7</sup> An application time of 30 minutes before venipuncture proved to be effective in a study involving children aged 1 to 4 years.<sup>8</sup> In this study, however, additional premedication, comprising a mixture of mefenamic acid, trimeprazine, and atropine, was administered to some of the children.

It was believed that 45 minutes after application was a reasonable time for analgesia to take effect based on previous data.<sup>6,7</sup> Side effects from topical application of EMLA range from local pallor to vasoconstriction, and they occur in approximately one third of all patients.<sup>9</sup>

These symptoms usually disappear within 24 hours. These side effects are not contraindications or allergic reactions. When used in large amounts, EMLA is contraindicated in children with known methemoglobinemia,<sup>10</sup> as it is suspected that the prilocaine in EMLA may cause or exacerbate methemoglobinemia.<sup>11</sup> In addition, EMLA should not be used with children to whom sulfonamides are being administered, as it has been suggested that sulfa may also contribute to methemoglobinemia.

## METHODS

An experimental post-test design was used in this double-blind study to compare pain levels between subjects who had EMLA applied topically before IV insertion and subjects who had placebo applied. A pretest design was not feasible. Convenience sampling was performed by selecting patients who needed IV insertion and were between the ages of 4 and 12 years. Convenience sampling was used, because the population being studied could not be selected randomly but was readily available for use in this study.<sup>12</sup> From this group, half of the subjects were randomly selected to have EMLA cream applied. Both the EMLA group and the placebo group were naïve to the type of cream being applied.

The subjects were male and female patients who were between the ages of 4 and 12 years and required IV access. Fifty-seven subjects were included in this study. Sex and race of the subjects were not considered when randomly assigning patients to either the EMLA group or the placebo group.

To be considered for participation in this study, a patient must have met the following acceptance criteria: (1) children aged 4 to 12 years; (2) English-speaking guardian(s) and children; (3) children who required insertion of an IV catheter; and (4) guardian(s) and children who agreed to participate in this study. Patients were excluded from the study if they met any of the following criteria: (1) children who had a previous allergic reaction to local anesthetics; (2) children with known methemoglobinemia; (3) children currently taking a sulfonamide medication; (4) non-English—speaking children or guardian(s), and (5) children who had mental disabilities.

Pain was scored using a 10-cm visual analogue scale (VAS) in conjunction with the Faces scale. The nurse’s evaluation of the child’s pain was assessed immediately after the IV catheter was inserted. The VAS scale and the Faces scale are both reliable and are used for different age groups.<sup>13</sup> The VAS scale measures pain severity on a vertical line 10 cm long. The child was asked to plot his/her pain immediately after the IV catheter was inserted along this line, with “0” being the least pain and “10” being the most painful or the worst pain ever. This scale is most useful in children aged 5 years and older.<sup>14</sup> The Faces scale measures pain intensity using a series of face drawings. A face with a smile represents the least pain or no pain, and a face with a frown and tears represents the most pain or the worst pain ever.<sup>15</sup> Combining these two scales served to address the range of ages studied. Informed consent was obtained from the legal guardian. It was believed that the child should also give his/her consent if he/she was able to understand the procedure that would directly involve him/her. The potential risks were explained to the patients and their guardians before the procedure.

The EMLA and placebo were packaged by the pharmacy staff, who placed exactly 2.5 g of either the EMLA or the placebo in an identical number of labeled syringes. The pharmacy staff prepared the creams and identified them in terms using numbers. The identification numbers were known only to the pharmacy staff and were unknown to the investigators until study completion. The placebo cream consisted of a white lotion that was similar in consistency to EMLA cream.

Forty-five minutes before catheter insertion, 2.5 g of cream was applied to a primary site and a secondary site. Only sites where the skin was intact were considered for application of the cream. The secondary site was to be used if the initial IV attempt was unsuccessful. The cream was covered with an occlusive dressing large enough so that the cream was entirely covered. The cream and the dressing remained in place and undisturbed for 45 minutes. After 45 minutes, both sites of application were prepared for the IV insertion. The dressings were removed just before catheter insertion. The sites of application were wiped with a 2 × 2-in gauze to clean away the cream. The areas were then wiped with an alcohol swab and allowed to air dry. A 22- or 24-gauge catheter needle was used to start the IV insertion. It was not known if any of the patients studied had prior experience with IV insertion. The children participating in the study were told that the cream applied might or might not decrease the pain associated with IV cannula insertion.

The patient was observed for 24 hours after application of the cream for signs of potential side effects. The guardian(s) and the patient were also informed of potential side effects of the cream and were instructed to inform the research nurse for any signs of adverse reactions observed after application.

## RESULTS

The 57 patients participating in the study were randomly divided into the placebo group (29 patients) and the EMLA group (28 patients). The groups then were compared with regard to sex, age, and race. The placebo group contained 10 boys and 19 girls, with a mean age of 8.1 years. In the placebo and EMLA groups, there was no substantial difference with regard to age, sex, or race. Table 1 compares the pain scores between the placebo group and the EMLA group with regard to sex, race, and age. None of the patients studied required a second attempt at IV insertion.

Pain scores were lower in the EMLA group compared with the placebo group. The mean score in the EMLA group was 1.25, and the mean score in the placebo group was 8.39 (p < 0.0001). These results correlated with the nurse’s visual observations, with the mean score for the EMLA group being 1.07 and that for the placebo group being 8.07 (p < 0.001).

All patients were observed for adverse side effects after insertion of the IV catheter. There were no reported side effects noted after the application of the EMLA cream. One patient in the placebo group experienced erythema at the application site. The erythema disappeared within 24 hours of application.

## DISCUSSION

Painful procedures for a child can be frightening, especially when there is an invasive procedure involving a needle as there is when IV insertion is required. The EMLA cream has been shown in this study to provide a noninvasive method of producing dermal anesthesia before insertion of an IV catheter in pediatric patients aged 4 to 12 years. As reported in previous studies with other procedures, EMLA has been shown to produce adequate analgesia effects in a relatively short amount of time. It was shown that when EMLA cream remained in place for at least 45 minutes, effective pain relief was obtained. This is important from a patient care perspective, because rapid analgesia (and, therefore, rapid IV insertion) allows therapeutic interventions to occur in a timely fashion. In addition, if a child is told that the needle insertion is painless, the child is likely to experience less anxiety during the insertion procedure, making it easier for the health care provider to insert the IV catheter.

Limitations of this study include the validity and reliability of using the Faces pain scale with the VAS pain scale. Separately, the two scales have been tested extensively and found to be reliable,<sup>16</sup> but no studies have tested the reliability of both scales used in conjunction. Because of the wide range of children being studied, it was believed that a combination of the two scales was needed to address the range of developmental abilities of the children being studied. Another limitation observed with regard to the measurement scales is the subjective interpretation of the VAS scale by the observing nurse. Additional studies are needed with regard to the scales used so that standardization and validity can be maintained.

## CONCLUSIONS

Lidocaine/prilocaine (EMLA) cream is effective in significantly reducing the pain associated with IV insertion compared with placebo. In addition, this effect is noted after only 45 minutes of application. No side effects of the lidocaine/prilocaine mixture were noted. This study showed that EMLA cream is a safe and effective product, decreasing pain 45 minutes after application in children who required IV insertion. Hospitals should be encouraged to have their practitioners make EMLA a “standing order” when a child requires insertion of an IV catheter in a nonemergency situation. This procedure would serve to facilitate pediatric IV access in a painless manner.
