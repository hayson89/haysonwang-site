# The use of EMLA reduces the pain associated with digital ring block for ingrowing toenail correction

J. Browne†, M. Fung†, M. Donnelly\* and C. Cooney\*

\*Department ofAnaesthesia, Meath/Adelaide Hospital, Tallaght, Dublin, Ireland and †Pain ReliefResearch Unit, Department ofAnaesthesia, King’s College Hospital, Denmark Hill, London, UK

## Summary

Treatment of ingrowing toenail is commonly performed under local anaesthesia with a digital ring block. Ring block can be associated with significant pain, caused by needle insertion and deposition of the local anaesthetic agent. EMLA, a topical anaesthetic cream, is widely used to alleviate the pain of venepuncture. The aim of this study was to investigate whether the discomfort of digital block could be reduced by prior application of EMLA. Fifty-five patients who were scheduled for ingrowing toenail under local anaesthesia were randomized into two groups.

## Introduction

Ingrowing toenail of the hallux is a common condition affecting mainly adolescents and young adults, with a 3 : 1 male predominance. It may be treated by a variety of practitioners. Various treatment options have been employed including nail edge/total nail avulsion and Zadiks procedure. However, wedge resection with segmental phenolization has been advocated as the optimal therapeutic approach [1].

Procedures are commonly performed under local anaesthesia using a ring block. Irrespective of what local anaesthetic agent is employed, ring block may be associated with significant pain [2], due to both needle injection and deposition of local anaesthetic solution. EMLA, a eutectic mixture of local anaesthetics, is a topical anaesthetic cream, which has been used in a large variety of clinical settings, but most commonly for venepuncture [3].

## Methods

Following institutional ethical committee approval and patient consent, patients scheduled for ingrowing toenail surgery under local anaesthesia were randomized into one of two groups. In Group 1, EMLA was applied as a thick layer (one full tube, 5 g) around the base of the affected hallux at the proposed sites of injection for at least 1 h prior to ring block and held in position securely with an adherent dressing. In Group 2, a placebo cream (moisturising cream that looked similar to EMLA) was applied in a similar manner. All patients had a digital ring block performed using a standardized technique. A total of 6 mL 0.5% plain bupivacaine was administered via a 25-gauge needle using two injections (3 mL × 2), one at either side of the base of the hallux. The same anaesthetist performed all the blocks. Immediately after the block, the pain caused was assessed using a 100-mm visual analogue pain score (VAPS). The trial was double blinded so neither the patient nor the assessor knew which cream had been applied.

The age and gender of the two groups were compared using an unpaired Student’s t-test and the $\chi ^ { 2 } -$ squared test, respectively. Differences in the VAPS of the two groups were assessed using the Mann Whitney U-test. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered significant.

## Results

The results are summarized in Table 1. Fifty-five patients were included in the study, 30 in Group 1 (EMLA) and 25 in Group 2 (placebo). There were no significant differences between the two groups with regard to age or gender. The mean (standard deviation) VAPS in Group 1 was 28.1 (10.74) and in Group 2, 50.1 (10.85) which was statistically significant $( P < 0 . 0 0 0 1 )$ . This represents a 40% decrease in the mean VAPS, which is also very relevant clinically. The 95% confidence interval for Group 1 was 24.6–33.4 and for Group 2, 45.6–54.4. We considered a VAPS of 40 to be unacceptable for ring block, as pain scores in excess of this value typically correspond to moderately severe pain on verbal scales. Patients in Group 2 consistently recorded scores greater than 40.

## Discussion

In our experience, the vast majority of operations for ingrowing toenail are performed using local anaesthesia. However, significant proportions of patients, especially younger patients, either request general anaesthesia or consent to local anaesthesia with reservation because of the pain associated with ring block. Depending on the type of operation performed there is a sizeable recurrence rate and therefore many patients remember the discomfort that the ring block entails.

In a previous study comparing ring blocks performed with various local anaesthetic agents, viz. lignocaine 1%, bupivacaine 0.5%, and bupivacaine 0.5% with hyaluronidase, the respective mean VAPS were 3.6, 5.1 and 5.3 (verbal rating score from 0 to 10). The authors recommended the use of bupivacaine, 0.5%, because of the prolonged analgesia it afforded [2]. These results are comparable to those in our trial. We conclude that the pain of conventional digit block may be significant and not surprisingly some patients do not wish to endure it. While most of these patients are young and the morbidity from general anaesthesia would reasonably be regarded as minimal, the inconvenience and associated costs could be relevant.

EMLA is a eutectic mixture of lignocaine 2.5% and prilocaine 2.5% in an oil/water emulsion. It has been commercially available in the UK since 1986. EMLA has been used to provide topical anaesthesia in a wide variety of clinical settings, most commonly venepuncture and intravenous cannulation. However, it has also been used for arterial cannulation, harvesting of smaller split skin grafts, lumbar puncture, post herpetic neuralgia, pulsed dye laser treatment of port wine stains and cauterization of genital condylomata, etc. [3,4]. The cream should be applied as a thick layer and held in situ with an adherent dressing for at least an hour [5].

The pain from ring block is caused by both needle insertion and deposition of local anaesthetic. Because of the sensitivity of the hallux and the tightness of the skin over the hallux, it is not difficult to appreciate why digital block can be painful. It would be reasonably expected that EMLA could alleviate the pain of the needle insertion. However, it is the injection of the local anaesthetic agent that causes most discomfort. If applied for long enough, the depth of analgesia obtainable from application of EMLA may be as much as 5 mm [6]. It is logical to assume that this may reduce but not abolish the pain of the local anaesthetic injection because the subcutaneous tissue will be anaesthetized down to a particular depth but not in the deeper regions. This concept may be reflected in the results reported here, i.e. the pain scores are significantly improved but some discomfort is still apparent.

Prior to the commencement of this trial, a nonrandomized, nonplacebo controlled pilot study with EMLA use for ring block was performed. The main problem encountered was ensuring that the adherent dressing remained in situ for 1 h. Because the skin contour around the base of both sides of the hallux make adherence more difficult than elsewhere, e.g. dorsum of hand, special care must be taken when applying the dressing, e.g. extra adherent dressings may be required. With this experience, no such problems were encountered in the trial.

In conclusion, the results of this trial indicate that conventional ring block is associated with significant discomfort which is reduced by the prior application of EMLA. We recommend the routine use of EMLA when performing a digital block, but caution that correct application is important.
