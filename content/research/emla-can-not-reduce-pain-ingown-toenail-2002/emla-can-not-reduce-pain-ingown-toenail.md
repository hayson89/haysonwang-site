Intact human skin can be anesthetized by applying a topical local anesthetic such as EMLA<sup>A</sup> cream (Astra, Södertälje, Sweden), permitting minor invasive procedures (2, 3).

The aim of this study was to evaluate the efficacy of EMLA cream prior to digital ring block for surgery of ingrown big toenail. Intra-operative and postoperative analgesia, psychological aspects of injection of anesthetic and surgery were not studied.

Eighty-one patients aged between 12 and 75 years (mean ∫ SD: 30.3 ∫ 17.5 years), who were scheduled for ingrown big toenail surgery, were randomly allocated to one of two treatment condition groups prior to enrollment using a computerized unbiased coin toss. Hospital Ethics Committee approval was granted and written informed consent was obtained from all patients, parents or guardians.

Anesthesia was given with digital nerve block, with previous application of EMLA cream (Group E) or a placebo cream (Group P). No patient received premedication, additional anesthesia or analgesia prior to the block. Patients’ data regarding age, gender, ethnic origin (Sepharadi or Ashkenazi Jew) and education level were recorded. The same investigator performed the anesthetic procedure throughout the study. EMLA or placebo cream (White Aqueous Cream, Floris Laboratory, Kiriat Bialik, Israel) was applied around the base of the big toe in a thick layer of one 5-g tube. The site was then covered with a plastic wrap (Steri-$\mathrm { D r a p e } ^ { \mathsf { T M } } ,$ , 3M Health care, St Paul, MN, USA), which was taped to the skin to form an adherent occlusive dressing. The application time, intended to be at least 1 h, was recorded. The digital block was performed immediately after the removal of the cream. It was administered to patients in the supine position, using a 25-gauge needle to inject a plain solution of 4 ml lidocaine 1% mixed with 4 ml bupivacaine 0.5% without epinephrine. The needle was introduced into the dorsal–lateral aspect of the proximal phalanx in the portion of the web space just distal to the metatarsal– phalangeal joint. The needle was advanced until it gently touched the bone, and 1.5 ml of anesthetic solution was delivered to the dorsal digital nerve. It was then withdrawn and redirected adjacent to the bone of the phalanx to the plantar surface of the toe and 2.5 ml of solution was injected continuously at the site of the plantar nerve. The procedure was repeated on the opposite side of the toe to achieve full anesthesia.

Evaluation of efficacy of the cream was performed throughout the study by the other investigator who, as well as the patients, was blinded to the nature of the cream employed. Patients were asked after the block had been performed to assess the pain both during the skin needle penetration and during the infiltration of the anesthetic solution, using a visual analog scale (VAS) according to Maunuksela & Korpela (2) and a verbal rating score (VRS) from 1 to 10, according to intensity of pain. Surgery involved partial resection of nail on its lateral and/or medial margin. In the postoperative period, patients were observed for local bleeding and signs of ischemia before discharge.

## Study design

The study was designed to have 85% power to detect a difference of 20% in VRS for both needle penetration into the skin and for infiltration of the anesthetic product, alpha Ω 0.05, one-sided. This produced a sample size of 40 participants for each treatment condition. Using the same calculation, a sample size of 37 was required for the VAS for both needle penetration and anesthetic infiltration. The final sample size was therefore 81 participants, randomized to treatment with EMLA or placebo.

## Data analysis

Data were stored on Excel 97 with Hebrew Language support (Microsoft Inc., Seattle, WA, USA 1985–97). Analysis of data was carried out using STATISTIX 4.0 statistical analysis software (La-Jolla, CA, USA, 1992).

For continuous variables such age, education level, VAS, VRS, and cream application time, descriptive statistics were calculated and are reported as mean ∫ standard deviation. Variables were assessed for normalcy of distribution. Age, application time and time of surgery had highly skewed distributions. Application time and time of surgery were compared by treatment condition using the Mann–Whitney nonparametric U-test. Distribution of age was normalized with log-transformation and, as with the remaining continuous variables, was compared by treatment group using the Student’s t-test for independent samples. Categorical variables such as treatment condition (EMLA or placebo), gender and ethnicity were described using frequency distributions and compared using the chi-square test.

## Results

The study included 81 patients assigned to Group E (n Ω 40) or Group P (n Ω 41). Table 1 shows the demographic characteristics of the study patients. No significant difference in gender, ethnic origin, or education level was found between the two groups. Because age differed significantly by treatment condition $( P = 0 . 0 0 6 )$ , it was included in all subsequent models of outcome variables.

No discomfort or complications were noted with EMLA or placebo cream. The duration of application of the cream until the local anesthetic procedure did not differ significantly between the two groups (Table 1).

VAS and VRS scores during the skin penetration and infiltration of the anesthetic solution are reported in Table 2. No significant difference was found between the EMLA and the placebo groups in pain rating or in the VAS and VRS ratios (Table 2). In Group E, gender had a significant effect on pain perception (Table 3). Male patients reported less pain than female patients, both during skin penetration and the infiltration step. This gender difference was not found in Group P (Table 3).

## Discussion

Minor surgical procedures are very frequently performed with cutaneous analgesia obtained by local injection of an anesthetic solution, which is a painful procedure. Pain occurs during needle penetration of the skin and during the infiltration and diffusion of the anesthetic product. Topical application of EMLA cream under an occlusive bandage has been shown to be safe and effective for local anesthesia in several procedures such as venipuncture, intradermal skin injections, vaccinations, minor surgery and laser therapy (2, 3). EMLA has also been recommended prior to spinal anesthesia in pediatric populations (4). However, Jones et al. (5) found no clinical benefit in the use of EMLA cream prior to percutaneous infiltration of lignocaine, most likely due to inadequate dermal anesthesia. In a previous study, we found that EMLA was not helpful for dorsal penile nerve block in children (6).

Contrary to the report of Browne et al. (7), we found that EMLA cream prior to digital nerve block of the big toe had no clinical benefit. We could not show a difference of \$20% between the two groups of patients in pain reduction. The application time of the cream (either EMLA or placebo) prior to the block was not significantly different between the two groups. The time between removal of the cream and the digital block was the same, as we performed the skin puncture immediately after removal of the cream.

Surprisingly, EMLA did not significantly reduce the pain of the initial needle insertion for digital block, as had been shown in other studies (3, 4, 6). Pain due to insertion of needles in intact skin is assumed to arise from direct pressure activation of the single nociceptors’ fibers, or from the release of prostaglandins or algesic peptides following needle-induced cellular damage (8). The cream had no effect on discomfort occurring during infiltration of the local anesthetic drug, because the depth of pure analgesia (i.e. block of pain sensation) provided by EMLA is limited (8). In addition, the discomfort of infiltration is associated with stimulation of nerve endings other than those of ‘pure pain’. The sensation arising from endings other than ‘pain endings’(e.g. those of touch, pressure, stretch) is more resistant to block by local analgesics, including EMLA; yet excessive stimulation of any of these endings may be interpreted as pain. As in the finger (9), the dorsal digital nerves of the toe are not deeply located in the small subcutaneous space, and topical anesthesia may not reach them. The two plantar digital nerves, more deeply located, are dominant and innervate the plantar surface of the toe, the whole plantar pad and nail bed area. Moreover, swelling due to injection of the anesthetic product in the small subcutaneous space of the digit may also amplify the sensation of pain. It has been shown that a minimally effective EMLA application time is 45 min (10), and that the optimal conditions (maximal depth of analgesia of approximately 5 mm) are obtained 30 min after a 90-min application time or during the 60-min period after a 120-min application (8) for both sensory and pain thresholds. For application times shorter than 120 min, the depth of analgesia increased during the period after removal of the cream (8). In a recent study (7), it was found that ‘EMLA significantly reduces the pain associated with digital ring block’. The cream was applied for at least 1 h prior to ring block, but mean time was not reported and no comparison between the two groups investigated was performed. Moreover, no distinction between pain due to needle insertion and pain due to infiltration was made, even though the authors discussed this essential point.

Among patients of Group E, females reported significantly more severe pain than males during the needle penetration as well as during the infiltration of the anesthetic product (Table 3). It is well known that the two genders have a different response to experimental pain stimuli. Robinson et al. (11) showed that in the group with local lidocaine treatment, males rated the stimuli as less painful than the females, while in the placebo control group, no sex differences were observed. In this study, we obtained similar results in the two groups.

In conclusion, we found no clinical benefit in using EMLA during big toe nerve block. Moreover, adding EMLA is time consuming and expensive. Some authors suggest that pain due to intradermal or subcutaneous administration of the anesthetic might be reduced by addition of sodium bicarbonate (12, 13), even in digital block (14, 15). Very slow injection of the local anesthetic (16), and injection of a small bolus of anesthetic to avoid distention of the tissues (17), might also reduce the pain associated with infiltration.
